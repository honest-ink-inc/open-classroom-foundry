# Article THIRD and the 501(c)(3) organizational test

**Date:** 11 September 2026 · **Kind:** verification record and counsel questions · **Status:** one bounded question open, referred to counsel

This records a verification that was performed, what it found, and the one question a reading cannot settle. It is research, not legal advice, and no one who prepared it is a lawyer. It grants nothing, clears nothing, and changes no gate.

## What was verified

The filed Articles of Incorporation were read directly — SDAT acknowledgment `5000000013523104`, filed and effective 29 August 2026, on form `SDAT02X.2`, *Articles of Incorporation for a Tax-Exempt Nonstock Corporation* rather than the plain nonstock form, which carries neither IRS clause. Both required clauses are present in that form's pre-printed text, as [the coordination plan](../pilots/human-gates-coordination-plan.md) records. Nothing distributes assets to members, directors, officers or private persons on dissolution, which matters because Maryland has no general statutory dedication to fall back on.

## The one question a reading cannot settle

Article THIRD has two parts, in this order, both inside the same article:

> **THIRD: The purpose(s) for which the corporation is formed is/are as follows:**
>
> *[typed by the incorporator]* Education & Training - Workforce development
>
> *[pre-printed]* Said corporation is organized exclusively for charitable, religious, educational, and scientific purposes, including, for such purposes, the making of distributions to organizations that qualify as exempt organizations under Section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, or the corresponding section of any future federal tax code.

Do those two sentences form **one** purpose clause, in which the pre-printed sentence limits the typed line — or **two** independent, co-equal purposes? Presence is a reading. Adequacy is not.

## Analysis

**The unit of analysis is the whole instrument.** Treas. Reg. § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(2) defines "articles of organization" as the corporate charter or any other written instrument by which an organization is created. The warning in Publication 557 and the Form 1023-EZ instructions — that a reference to § 501(c)(3) does not by itself limit purposes and that *all* the language is considered — is the same rule, and it cuts both ways rather than only against the filer.

**Two conjunctive requirements, and only one is at issue.** § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(i) requires that the articles *(a)* limit purposes to one or more exempt purposes, and *(b)* not expressly empower more than insubstantial non-exempt activity. **Article EIGHTH answers *(b)*, not *(a)*.** Its "Notwithstanding any other provision" sentence limits **activities**, and the IRS's own training material states that a clause limiting activities "would not cure an overly broad purpose statement." Any argument that leans on EIGHTH for this question is leaning on the wrong article; the work is done by THIRD's second sentence.

**A narrowing descriptor is not the defect the regulation targets.** § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(ii) permits stated purposes "as broad as, or more specific than" those in § 501(c)(3). § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(1)(iv) bars only purposes *broader than* § 501(c)(3)'s, and further provides that actual operations and members' intent cannot rescue a defective clause — it is the adverse rule, not a savings provision.

**"Not one of the eight enumerated purposes" is not the failure test.** The regulation's own passing example is the operation of a school for adult education, which is not enumerated either. "Training" is the regulation's own word for what *educational* means, at § 1.501(c)(3)-1(d)(3)(i)(a).

**Even the most aggressive reading names an exempt purpose.** Rev. Rul. 73-127 holds in terms that providing job training for the hard-core unemployed "is charitable and educational within the meaning of … section 501(c)(3)"; that ruling denied exemption for a *grocery store* held to be a second, independent, non-charitable objective, not for the training. Rev. Rul. 73-128 reaches the same conclusion on the training side, and Rev. Rul. 76-37 holds a vocational-training support organization operated exclusively for charitable purposes. All three are operational-test rulings about conduct rather than organizational-test rulings about drafting, and should be cited for substance only.

**The document's shape matches the IRS's passing pattern.** Its training material pairs failures with cures: "formed to operate a school" fails, "formed to operate a school for educational purposes" satisfies. Description plus exclusivity-in-statutory-terms is the passing shape, and that is what Article THIRD produces by design.

## The argument against, stated at its strongest

An undefined added term can spoil a clause that also contains proper ones. The IRS's 1985 training material fails purposes stated as "charitable, philanthropic, and benevolent" because *philanthropic* and *benevolent* "have no generally accepted legal meaning and, therefore, the stated purposes may, under state law, permit activities that are broader than those intended." **"Workforce development" is likewise not a term of art** in § 501(c)(3) or its regulations. The distinguishing feature is real — that example had no "exclusively" limitation following it, and this one does.

Separately, **the instrument contains no purposes-directed "notwithstanding" clause**, which is what the IRS's stated safe harbour uses. Maryland's form does not supply one. THIRD's second sentence limits purposes but does not expressly override the line above it.

And the governing construction rule is Maryland's: § 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(5) makes the law of the state of creation controlling in construing the articles. No Maryland decision construing this form was located. Md. Code, Com. Law § 3-114 (typewritten prevails over printed) is confined to negotiable instruments. Contra proferentem under *Impac Mortgage Holdings, Inc. v. Timm* is a last-resort canon that would run against the incorporator who typed the line, not against the State that printed the form.

## Questions for counsel

1. Under Maryland law, do the two sentences of Article THIRD form a single purpose clause in which the pre-printed sentence limits the typed line, or two independent, co-equal purposes? This is the hinge.
2. Does the absence of any purposes-directed "notwithstanding" clause matter, given that THIRD's second sentence is itself a purposes limitation in statutory terms?
3. Is "Education & Training - Workforce development" better characterised as a limiting purpose, a descriptive gloss, or a filing-category label — and does the characterisation change the answer? Md. Code, Corps. & Ass'ns § 2-104(a)(3) required stated purposes and the incorporator supplied them rather than electing any lawful business, which cuts against dismissing the line as decoration.
4. Is there material risk that a reviewer reads "Workforce development" as a second, independent, non-exempt objective on the model of Rev. Rul. 73-127 — and does that ruling's own holding foreclose it?
5. Should the organization raise this proactively in response to any IRS information request, or say nothing unless asked?
6. If an amendment were filed now, is a clarifying restatement of THIRD nonsubstantive under Publication 557, so that recognition still runs from 29 August 2026 — and does filing while the application is pending create a characterisation question that not filing avoids?
7. Please read the certified copy and confirm: that the typed text landed in THIRD's blank; that the pre-printed exclusivity sentence appears in THIRD unaltered; that Article EIGHTH matches the form's pre-printed text with nothing added; and that no rider displaced anything. 8. Confirm what was attested on Form 1023-EZ about the organizing document, and whether any mismatch creates exposure independent of the organizational test.

## Recommendation

**Do not file a prophylactic amendment now.** The money is trivial — Maryland Articles of Amendment are $100, or $150 expedited, 4–6 weeks or 7–10 business days. The reason to wait is that Publication 557 makes a *substantive* amendment move the determination's effective date to the date of the change, while a nonsubstantive one preserves date-of-formation recognition. A clarifying restatement is very likely nonsubstantive, but filing it mid-determination invites a characterisation question that not filing never raises.

**There is no deadline.** The 27-month rule in Rev. Proc. 2026-5 § 6.09(1) governs when the *application* is filed, and Form 1023-EZ is already filed. Nothing is closing. An earlier statement in this repository that the question sat on a clock was wrong and is withdrawn.

**Amend if** the IRS raises anything touching Article THIRD — then amend as the response; or counsel's own reading of the certified copy finds the typed text misplaced, a rider present, or EIGHTH altered; or counsel concludes the two sentences do not form one clause; or an unrelated amendment becomes necessary anyway, in which case bundle the fix and pay once.

## Proportion

Form 1023-EZ filers do not submit organizing documents, so the IRS ordinarily never sees them; Rev. Proc. 2026-5 § 6.07(2)(b) provides for a statistically valid random sample of pre-determination reviews. The National Taxpayer Advocate found that among a representative sample of corporations whose Form 1023-EZ the IRS **approved**, the articles of 37 percent did not satisfy the organizational test. This document is materially better than that median, which is context rather than comfort.

The tail worth naming: if an inaccurate organizing-document attestation survived into a determination letter, that letter may not be relied upon and revocation can be retroactive to its effective date. That tail exists only if nobody looks and nobody cures. **No determination letter has issued, so nothing has crystallised.**
